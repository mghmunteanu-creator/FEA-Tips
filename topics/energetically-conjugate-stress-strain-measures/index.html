01

Introduction: from force and displacement to stress and strain

In elementary mechanics, the infinitesimal work performed by a force FFF during a displacement dududu is:

dW=F dudW=F\,dudW=Fdu

The force and displacement are called energetically conjugate quantities: their product gives mechanical work.

In elasticity, we use the same idea, but replace force and displacement with stress and strain.

For an infinitesimal deformation of a material element, the internal mechanical work is related to a stress tensor and an appropriate strain increment.

At small strains, this relationship is familiar:

dw=σ:dεdw=\boldsymbol{\sigma}:d\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}dw=σ:dε

Here, the double contraction of the two second-order tensors can be written explicitly using their 3 × 3 matrix representations:

σ:dε=[σxxσxyσxzσyxσyyσyzσzxσzyσzz]:[dεxxdεxydεxzdεyxdεyydεyzdεzxdεzydεzz]\boldsymbol{\sigma}:d\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}=\left[ \begin{matrix}{\sigma }_{xx}&{\sigma }_{xy}&{\sigma }_{xz}\\{\sigma }_{yx}&{\sigma }_{yy}&{\sigma }_{yz}\\{\sigma }_{zx}&{\sigma }_{zy}&{\sigma }_{zz}\end{matrix}\right]:\left[ \begin{matrix}d{\varepsilon }_{xx}&d{\varepsilon }_{xy}&d{\varepsilon }_{xz}\\d{\varepsilon }_{yx}&d{\varepsilon }_{yy}&d{\varepsilon }_{yz}\\d{\varepsilon }_{zx}&d{\varepsilon }_{zy}&d{\varepsilon }_{zz}\end{matrix}\right]σ:dε=​σxx​σyx​σzx​​σxy​σyy​σzy​​σxz​σyz​σzz​​​:​dεxx​dεyx​dεzx​​dεxy​dεyy​dεzy​​dεxz​dεyz​dεzz​​​

In Cartesian coordinates, the same operation is the sum of the products of corresponding components:

σ:dε=∑i=13∑j=13σij dεij\boldsymbol{\sigma}:d\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}=\sum_{i=1}^{3} \sum_{j=1}^{3} {\sigma }_{ij}\,d{\varepsilon }_{ij}σ:dε=i=1∑3​j=1∑3​σij​dεij​

Since both the Cauchy stress and the infinitesimal strain tensors are symmetric, the expression can also be expanded as:

σ:dε=σxx dεxx+σyy dεyy+σzz dεzz+2(σxy dεxy+σyz dεyz+σzx dεzx)\boldsymbol{\sigma}:d\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}={\sigma }_{xx}\,d{\varepsilon }_{xx}+{\sigma }_{yy}\,d{\varepsilon }_{yy}+{\sigma }_{zz}\,d{\varepsilon }_{zz}+2\left( {\sigma }_{xy}\,d{\varepsilon }_{xy}+{\sigma }_{yz}\,d{\varepsilon }_{yz}+{\sigma }_{zx}\,d{\varepsilon }_{zx}\right)σ:dε=σxx​dεxx​+σyy​dεyy​+σzz​dεzz​+2(σxy​dεxy​+σyz​dεyz​+σzx​dεzx​)

The factor 2 in the shear terms follows from tensor symmetry: each off-diagonal contribution appears twice. The strain components used here are tensorial components, not engineering shear strains.

The distinction becomes important when large deformations and rotations are considered.

There are several possible stress and strain measures. Although each has a well-defined mechanical meaning, not every stress measure is energetically conjugate to the ordinary increment of every strain measure [1], [2].

This section considers only two descriptions:

  • Green–Lagrange strain and Second Piola–Kirchhoff stress, expressed in the initial configuration.
  • Euler–Almansi strain and Cauchy stress, expressed in the current configuration.

The purpose is to show how both descriptions represent the same internal mechanical work.

02

Two configurations of the same body

Following the notation adopted in Section 17.1 of FEA Tips, let a material point occupy the position x\boldsymbol{x}x in the initial configuration and the position x^\widehat{\boldsymbol{x}}x in the current configuration. The total displacement is:

x^=x+u\widehat{\boldsymbol{x}}=\boldsymbol{x}+\boldsymbol{u}x=x+u

Thus:

F=I+g\boldsymbol{F}=\boldsymbol{I}+\boldsymbol{g}F=I+g

In linear elasticity, the infinitesimal strain tensor has the familiar expression:

Throughout this section, ε\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}ε denotes the infinitesimal strain tensor, whereas E\boldsymbol{E}E and e\boldsymbol{e}e denote the Green–Lagrange and Euler–Almansi strain tensors. All three are second-order tensors; the different letters identify different strain measures, not different tensor orders.

The symbol g\boldsymbol gg denotes the gradient of the total displacement with respect to the initial coordinates x\boldsymbol xx. Its hatted counterpart g^\widehat{\boldsymbol g}g​ denotes the gradient of an infinitesimal additional displacement dud\boldsymbol udu, evaluated with respect to the current coordinates x^\widehat{\boldsymbol x}x.

g^=∂(du)∂x^\widehat{\boldsymbol{g}}=\frac{\partial \left( d\boldsymbol{u}\right)}{\partial \widehat{\boldsymbol{x}}}g​=∂x∂(du)​

These notation choices follow the initial/current distinction used in Section 17.1.

The corresponding volume elements satisfy:

dV^=J dV,J=det⁡F>0d\widehat{V}=J\,dV,\quad \quad J=\det \boldsymbol{F}>0dV=JdV,J=detF>0

We shall use two descriptions of the same deformation.

In the Total Lagrangian description, the reference configuration remains fixed.

In the Updated Lagrangian description, the reference configuration changes as the body deforms. Consequently, tensors expressed in the current configuration must account for the changing geometry and orientation of the material.

This distinction is essential for understanding energetic conjugacy [1], [2].

03

Green–Lagrange strain and Second Piola–Kirchhoff stress

The Green–Lagrange strain tensor is:

E=12(FTF−I)\boldsymbol{E}=\frac{1}{2}\left( {\boldsymbol{F}}^{T}\boldsymbol{F}-\boldsymbol{I}\right)E=21​(FTF−I)

The Second Piola–Kirchhoff stress tensor S\boldsymbol{S}S is defined with respect to the initial configuration.

For an infinitesimal change in deformation, the internal mechanical work per unit initial volume is:

dw0=S:dEd{w}_{0}=\boldsymbol{S}:d\boldsymbol{E}dw0​=S:dE

The subscript 000 in dw0dw_0dw0​ denotes work per unit initial volume.

Thus, Second Piola–Kirchhoff stress and Green–Lagrange strain are energetically conjugate [1], [2].

This relationship is particularly convenient because both tensors are referred to the same fixed initial configuration.

There is no need to update the reference configuration during deformation.

04

Euler–Almansi strain and Cauchy stress

The situation is more subtle in the current configuration.

The Euler–Almansi strain tensor is:

e=12(I−F−TF−1)\boldsymbol{e}=\frac{1}{2}\left( \boldsymbol{I}-{\boldsymbol{F}}^{-T}{\boldsymbol{F}}^{-1}\right)e=21​(I−F−TF−1)

Unlike Green–Lagrange strain, Euler–Almansi strain is expressed in the current spatial configuration.

Cauchy stress σ\boldsymbol{\sigma}σ is also expressed in this configuration. It represents the actual force per unit area acting on a surface in the deformed body.

At first sight, it would seem natural to write:

dwsp=σ:ded{w}_{sp}=\boldsymbol{\sigma}:d\boldsymbol{e}dwsp​=σ:de

The differential of the Euler–Almansi strain tensor is derived in detail in Section 31.1a.

However, this is not the general expression for internal mechanical work at finite deformations [1], [2].

The difficulty is that the current configuration changes continuously. The ordinary increment ded\boldsymbol{e}de contains effects associated with the changing spatial description.

We must distinguish it from the infinitesimal strain increment that produces internal work.

Infinitesimal displacement from the current configuration

Consider a very small additional displacement du=(dudvdw)Td\boldsymbol{u}={\left( \begin{matrix}du&dv&dw\end{matrix}\right)}^{T}du=(du​dv​dw​)T, measured from the current configuration x^\widehat{\boldsymbol{x}}x. Define its spatial gradient:

g^=∂(du)∂x^org^=[∂(du)∂x^∂(du)∂y^∂(du)∂z^∂(dv)∂x^∂(dv)∂y^∂(dv)∂z^∂(dw)∂x^∂(dw)∂y^∂(dw)∂z^]\widehat{\boldsymbol{g}}=\frac{\partial \left( d\boldsymbol{u}\right)}{\partial \widehat{\boldsymbol{x}}}\qquad\text{or}\qquad \widehat{\boldsymbol{g}}= \left[ \begin{matrix}\frac{\partial \left( du\right)}{\partial \widehat{x}}&\frac{\partial \left( du\right)}{\partial \widehat{y}}&\frac{\partial \left( du\right)}{\partial \widehat{z}}\\\frac{\partial \left( dv\right)}{\partial \widehat{x}}&\frac{\partial \left( dv\right)}{\partial \widehat{y}}&\frac{\partial \left( dv\right)}{\partial \widehat{z}}\\\frac{\partial \left( dw\right)}{\partial \widehat{x}}&\frac{\partial \left( dw\right)}{\partial \widehat{y}}&\frac{\partial \left( dw\right)}{\partial \widehat{z}}\end{matrix}\right]g​=∂x∂(du)​org​=​∂x∂(du)​∂x∂(dv)​∂x∂(dw)​​∂y​∂(du)​∂y​∂(dv)​∂y​∂(dw)​​∂z∂(du)​∂z∂(dv)​∂z∂(dw)​​​

The hatted symbol denotes the incremental displacement gradient in the current configuration. It must not be confused with the total displacement gradient defined in Subsection 02, which is referred to the initial configuration.

The associated infinitesimal spatial strain increment is:

dεsp=12(g^+g^T)d{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}_{sp}=\frac{1}{2}\left( \widehat{\boldsymbol{g}}+{\widehat{\boldsymbol{g}}}^{T}\right)dεsp​=21​(g​+g​T)

The subscript spspsp stands for spatial: dεspd{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}_{sp}dεsp​ is the infinitesimal strain increment calculated with respect to the current coordinates x^\widehat{\boldsymbol{x}}x. It is not the ordinary differential ded\boldsymbol{e}de of the total Euler–Almansi strain. The symbol ε\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}ε emphasizes its familiar infinitesimal-strain form.

This is the familiar formula from linear elasticity, but its derivatives are now evaluated with respect to the current coordinates.

The additional displacement is infinitesimal, even though the deformation already accumulated by the body may be large.

The internal mechanical work per unit current volume is:

dwsp=σ:dεspd{w}_{sp}=\boldsymbol{\sigma}:d{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}_{sp}dwsp​=σ:dεsp​

Thus, Cauchy stress is energetically conjugate to the infinitesimal spatial strain increment.

05

Euler–Almansi and the infinitesimal spatial strain increment

We now seek a relationship between the ordinary increment ded\boldsymbol{e}de and the spatial strain increment dεspd{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}_{sp}dεsp​.

For the infinitesimal additional displacement, the deformation gradient changes according to:

Fnew=(I+g^)F{\boldsymbol{F}}_{new}=\left( \boldsymbol{I}+\widehat{\boldsymbol{g}}\right)\boldsymbol{F}Fnew​=(I+g​)F

The Euler-Almansi strain is:

e=12(I−F−TF−1)\boldsymbol{e}=\frac{1}{2}\left( \boldsymbol{I}-{\boldsymbol{F}}^{-T}{\boldsymbol{F}}^{-1}\right)e=21​(I−F−TF−1)

Define the symmetric tensor:

B=F−TF−1=I−2e\boldsymbol{B}={\boldsymbol{F}}^{-T}{\boldsymbol{F}}^{-1}=\boldsymbol{I}-2\boldsymbol{e}B=F−TF−1=I−2e

Neglecting terms of second order in the infinitesimal increment g^\widehat{\boldsymbol{g}}g​:

The new matrix B\boldsymbol BB, after applying the very small additional displacement dud\boldsymbol udu, is:

Bnew=(I−g^T)B(I−g^){\boldsymbol{B}}_{new}=\left( \boldsymbol{I}-{\widehat{\boldsymbol{g}}}^{T}\right)\boldsymbol{B}\left( \boldsymbol{I}-\widehat{\boldsymbol{g}}\right)Bnew​=(I−g​T)B(I−g​)

Hence, neglecting second-order infinitesimal terms:

de=enew−e=12(I−Bnew)−12(I−B)=12(g^TB+Bg^)d\boldsymbol{e}={\boldsymbol{e}}_{new}-\boldsymbol e=\frac{1}{2}\left( \boldsymbol{I}-{\boldsymbol{B}}_{new}\right)-\frac{1}{2}\left( \boldsymbol{I}-\boldsymbol B\right)=\frac{1}{2}\left( {\widehat{\boldsymbol{g}}}^{T}\boldsymbol{B}+\boldsymbol{B}\widehat{\boldsymbol{g}}\right)de=enew​−e=21​(I−Bnew​)−21​(I−B)=21​(g​TB+Bg​)

Substituting B=I−2e\boldsymbol{B}=\boldsymbol{I}-2\boldsymbol{e}B=I−2e:

de=12(g^T+g^)−g^Te−eg^d\boldsymbol{e}=\frac{1}{2}\left( {\widehat{\boldsymbol{g}}}^{T}+\widehat{\boldsymbol{g}}\right)-{\widehat{\boldsymbol{g}}}^{T}\boldsymbol{e}-\boldsymbol{e}\widehat{\boldsymbol{g}}de=21​(g​T+g​)−g​Te−eg​

But:

dεsp=12(g^T+g^)d{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}_{sp}=\frac{1}{2}\left( {\widehat{\boldsymbol{g}}}^{T}+\widehat{\boldsymbol{g}}\right)dεsp​=21​(g​T+g​)

Therefore:

dεsp=de+g^Te+eg^d{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}_{sp}=d\boldsymbol{e}+{\widehat{\boldsymbol{g}}}^{T}\boldsymbol{e}+\boldsymbol{e}\widehat{\boldsymbol{g}}dεsp​=de+g​Te+eg​

This equation is fundamental.

The ordinary increment ded\boldsymbol{e}de is supplemented by two terms that account for the changing spatial configuration.

This result is valid to first order in the additional displacement, irrespective of the magnitude of the deformation already accumulated [1], [2]. The two additional terms account for the changing current configuration.

The important consequence is that this corrected Euler–Almansi increment, rather than its ordinary increment, enters the internal mechanical work expressed with Cauchy stress.

Consequently, the internal work per unit current volume is:

dwsp=σ:dεspd{w}_{sp}=\boldsymbol{\sigma}:d{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}_{sp}dwsp​=σ:dεsp​

06

Both formulations give the same internal work

We can now establish the connection between the Total and Updated Lagrangian descriptions.

The Green–Lagrange strain is:

E=12(FTF−I)\boldsymbol{E}=\frac{1}{2}\left( {\boldsymbol{F}}^{T}\boldsymbol{F}-\boldsymbol{I}\right)E=21​(FTF−I)

For the infinitesimal additional deformation:

Fnew=(I+g^)F{\boldsymbol{F}}_{new}=\left( \boldsymbol{I}+\widehat{\boldsymbol{g}}\right)\boldsymbol{F}Fnew​=(I+g​)F

Therefore, neglecting second-order infinitesimal terms:

dE=Enew−E=12FT(g^T+g^)Fd\boldsymbol{E}={\boldsymbol{E}}_{new}-\boldsymbol{E}=\frac{1}{2}{\boldsymbol{F}}^{T}\left( {\widehat{\boldsymbol{g}}}^{T}+\widehat{\boldsymbol{g}}\right)\boldsymbol{F}dE=Enew​−E=21​FT(g​T+g​)F

Consequently:

dE=FTdεspFd\boldsymbol{E}={\boldsymbol{F}}^{T}d{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}_{sp}\boldsymbol{F}dE=FTdεsp​F

This relates the strain increments in the two descriptions.

The corresponding stress measures are related by:

σ=1JFSFT\boldsymbol{\sigma}=\frac{1}{J}\boldsymbol{FS}{\boldsymbol{F}}^{T}σ=J1​FSFT

Substituting these relationships into the Total Lagrangian work expression:

S:dE=S:(FT dεsp F)=(FSFT):dεsp=Jσ:dεsp\boldsymbol{S}:d\boldsymbol{E}=\boldsymbol{S}:\left( {\boldsymbol{F}}^{T}\,d{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}_{sp}\,\boldsymbol{F}\right)=\left( \boldsymbol{FS}{\boldsymbol{F}}^{T}\right):d{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}_{sp}=J\boldsymbol{\sigma}:d{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}_{sp}S:dE=S:(FTdεsp​F)=(FSFT):dεsp​=Jσ:dεsp​

Thus:

S:dE=Jσ:dεsp\boldsymbol{S}:d\boldsymbol{E}=J\boldsymbol{\sigma}:d{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}_{sp}S:dE=Jσ:dεsp​

Since dV^=J dVd\widehat{V}=J\,dVdV=JdV, the internal mechanical work over the body is identical:

dWint=∫VS:dE dV=∫V^σ:dεsp dV^d{W}_{int}=\int_{V} \boldsymbol{S}:d\boldsymbol{E}\,dV=\int_{\widehat{V}} \boldsymbol{\sigma}:d{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}_{sp}\,d\widehat{V}dWint​=∫V​S:dEdV=∫V​σ:dεsp​dV

This is the central result of energetic conjugacy [1], [2].

The two formulations use different stress measures, strain increments and volume measures, but describe the same internal mechanical work.

07

A simple uniaxial example

Consider a homogeneous deformation with stretch ratio λ\lambda λ in the longitudinal direction and unchanged transverse dimensions:

F=[λ00010001]\boldsymbol{F}=\left[ \begin{matrix}\lambda &0&0\\0&1&0\\0&0&1\end{matrix}\right]F=​λ00​010​001​​

The volume ratio is:

J=λJ=\lambda J=λ

The longitudinal Green–Lagrange strain is:

E=12(λ2−1)E=\frac{1}{2}\left( {\lambda }^{2}-1\right)E=21​(λ2−1)

Its infinitesimal increment is:

dE=λ dλdE=\lambda \,d\lambda dE=λdλ

The longitudinal Euler–Almansi strain is:

e=12(1−λ−2)e=\frac{1}{2}\left( 1-{\lambda }^{-2}\right)e=21​(1−λ−2)

But the spatial strain increment is:

The two increments differ by 44% relative to dedede. They cannot be interchanged at finite deformation.

The longitudinal stress transformation gives:

σ=λS\sigma =\lambda Sσ=λS

The internal work per unit initial volume is:

S dE=Sλ dλ=σ dλS\,dE=S\lambda \,d\lambda =\sigma \,d\lambda SdE=Sλdλ=σdλ

The example also illustrates why the ordinary Euler–Almansi increment ded\boldsymbol{e}de cannot simply replace the spatial increment.

08

Connection with hyperelastic materials

For a hyperelastic material, the stress response is derived from a strain-energy function [2], [3].

Let W0{W}_{0}W0​ denote the stored strain energy per unit initial volume.

If this energy is expressed as a function of Green–Lagrange strain, its infinitesimal change is:

dW0=S:dEd{W}_{0}=\boldsymbol{S}:d\boldsymbol{E}dW0​=S:dE

The subscript 000 in dW0dW_0dW0​ means that the work is expressed per unit initial volume.

Consequently:

S=∂W0∂E\boldsymbol{S}=\frac{\partial {W}_{0}}{\partial \boldsymbol{E}}S=∂E∂W0​​

The corresponding Cauchy stress follows from:

σ=1JFSFT\boldsymbol{\sigma}=\frac{1}{J}\boldsymbol{FS}{\boldsymbol{F}}^{T}σ=J1​FSFT

This procedure ensures that the stress measures remain consistent with the energy function.

By contrast, directly assuming a linear relationship between Cauchy stress and total Euler–Almansi strain does not, by itself, establish a finite-strain hyperelastic material law.

This distinction is important when moving from geometrically nonlinear elastic FEM formulations to hyperelastic constitutive models.

In the next section, the Saint-Venant–Kirchhoff material provides a particularly simple example. Its strain-energy function is quadratic in Green–Lagrange strain, and its Second Piola–Kirchhoff stress follows directly by differentiation.

The Green–Lagrange formulation is especially straightforward because its reference configuration is fixed.

The Euler–Almansi formulation requires greater care because its spatial configuration changes with deformation.

As discussed in Section 17.1, polar decomposition separates rigid-body rotation from stretching. The relation derived in Subsection 05 connects the ordinary Euler–Almansi increment to the infinitesimal spatial increment used to evaluate internal work.

Energetic conjugacy is therefore not merely a choice of notation: it ensures that stress and strain measures describe the same physical mechanical work.

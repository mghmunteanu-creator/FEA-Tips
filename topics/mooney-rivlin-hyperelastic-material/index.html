01

Introduction

FormulationThree-dimensional (3D)
KinematicsLarge displacements and large strains
MaterialNonlinear elastic, isotropic
ResponseNearly incompressible

The Total Lagrangian (TL) formulation is adopted, consistently with Section 18.1, Section 18.2, Section 18.3, the two-dimensional formulations in Section 12.1, Section 12.2, Section 12.3 and the three-dimensional formulations in Section 26.1 and Section 26.2.

Hyperelasticity describes an ideal elastic response characterized by a stored strain-energy density function. For the corresponding elastic model, stress is determined by the current deformation rather than the particular loading path. Elastomers such as rubber and many polymers or soft tissues motivate the use of finite-strain hyperelastic material laws [2–4]. A hyperelastic law, however, does not automatically fit any material at arbitrarily large deformation.

As established in Section 31.1, when the energy per unit initial volume is expressed in terms of Green–Lagrange strain, the energetically conjugate Second Piola–Kirchhoff stress is:

S=∂W0∂E\boldsymbol{S}=\frac{\partial {W}_{0}}{\partial \boldsymbol{E}}S=∂E∂W0​​

Here W0{W}_{0}W0​ is the energy density, S\boldsymbol{S}S is the Second Piola–Kirchhoff stress and E\boldsymbol{E}E is the Green–Lagrange strain. The index 000 indicates reference to the initial volume.

The Mooney–Rivlin model is one of the most widely used hyperelastic constitutive models, especially in modelling rubber-like materials. This section develops its nearly incompressible version and illustrates it with a three-dimensional finite-element example.

02

03

Mooney–Rivlin energy and near incompressibility

The simple two-parameter incompressible Mooney–Rivlin energy is commonly written [2–4]:

W0=A10(I1−3)+A01(I2−3),J=1{W}_{0}={A}_{10}\left( {I}_{1}-3\right)+{A}_{01}\left( {I}_{2}-3\right),\quad \quad J=1W0​=A10​(I1​−3)+A01​(I2​−3),J=1

Here A10{A}_{10}A10​ and A01{A}_{01}A01​ are material constants. The model describes substantial reversible deformation in many rubber-like materials, but its range of validity depends on experimental calibration and loading mode; a single universal strain limit should not be assumed.

For nearly incompressible behaviour, the volumetric and distortional parts are separated. The first two ordinary invariants depend not only on distortion but also on volume change. Following [3], as in Section 18.1, Section 18.2, Section 18.3, introduce the reduced invariants:

Iˉ1=I1I3−1/3=J−2/3I1\bar I_1=I_1I_3^{-1/3}=J^{-2/3}I_1Iˉ1​=I1​I3−1/3​=J−2/3I1​
Iˉ2=I2I3−2/3=J−4/3I2\bar I_2=I_2I_3^{-2/3}=J^{-4/3}I_2Iˉ2​=I2​I3−2/3​=J−4/3I2​
Iˉ3=I31/2=J\bar I_3=I_3^{1/2}=JIˉ3​=I31/2​=J

The first two reduced invariants, Iˉ1\bar I_1Iˉ1​ and Iˉ2\bar I_2Iˉ2​, are introduced to separate the distortional and volumetric contributions to deformation. They remain unchanged under pure volumetric deformation, while Iˉ3=J\bar I_3=JIˉ3​=J measures the local volume ratio. This decomposition allows the strain-energy function to account independently for changes in shape and volume.

The auxiliary notation used in the constitutive derivations and MATLAB routines is equivalent:

J1=Iˉ1,J2=Iˉ2,J3=Iˉ3=JJ_1=\bar I_1,\qquad J_2=\bar I_2,\qquad J_3=\bar I_3=JJ1​=Iˉ1​,J2​=Iˉ2​,J3​=Iˉ3​=J

The strain-energy density is [3, 4]:

W0=Wdist+Wvol{W}_{0}={W}_{dist}+{W}_{vol}W0​=Wdist​+Wvol​
W0=A10(J1−3)+A01(J2−3)+K2(J3−1)2{W}_{0}={A}_{10}\left( {J}_{1}-3\right)+{A}_{01}\left( {J}_{2}-3\right)+\frac{K}{2}{\left( {J}_{3}-1\right)}^{2}W0​=A10​(J1​−3)+A01​(J2​−3)+2K​(J3​−1)2

The first two terms describe distortion; the final term penalizes volume change. Here KKK is the bulk modulus. The penalty formulation models near incompressibility rather than imposing J=1J=1J=1 identically.

For small strains, this energy gives the initial shear modulus:

G=2(A10+A01)G=2\left( {A}_{10}+{A}_{01}\right)G=2(A10​+A01​)

The bulk modulus of an isotropic material is related to the small-strain Young modulus EY{E}_{Y}EY​ and Poisson ratio ν\nuν by:

K=EY3(1−2ν)K=\frac{{E}_{Y}}{3\left( 1-2\nu \right)}K=3(1−2ν)EY​​

When ν\nuν approaches 0.50.50.5, KKK becomes much greater than GGG. This difference in stiffness is one of the reasons why nearly incompressible finite-element calculations require care (see Section 18.3).

04

Second Piola–Kirchhoff stress

The constitutive equation is obtained directly from the energy:

S=∂W0∂E=A10∂J1∂E+A01∂J2∂E+K(J3−1)∂J3∂E\boldsymbol{S}=\frac{\partial {W}_{0}}{\partial \boldsymbol{E}}={A}_{10}\frac{\partial {J}_{1}}{\partial \boldsymbol{E}}+{A}_{01}\frac{\partial {J}_{2}}{\partial \boldsymbol{E}}+K\left( {J}_{3}-1\right)\frac{\partial {J}_{3}}{\partial \boldsymbol{E}}S=∂E∂W0​​=A10​∂E∂J1​​+A01​∂E∂J2​​+K(J3​−1)∂E∂J3​​

The required first derivatives of the reduced invariants follow by the product rule:

∂J1∂E=I3−1/3∂I1∂E−13I1I3−4/3∂I3∂E\frac{\partial {J}_{1}}{\partial \boldsymbol{E}}={I}_{3}^{-1/3}\frac{\partial {I}_{1}}{\partial \boldsymbol{E}}-\frac{1}{3}{I}_{1}{I}_{3}^{-4/3}\frac{\partial {I}_{3}}{\partial \boldsymbol{E}}∂E∂J1​​=I3−1/3​∂E∂I1​​−31​I1​I3−4/3​∂E∂I3​​
∂J2∂E=I3−2/3∂I2∂E−23I2I3−5/3∂I3∂E\frac{\partial {J}_{2}}{\partial \boldsymbol{E}}={I}_{3}^{-2/3}\frac{\partial {I}_{2}}{\partial \boldsymbol{E}}-\frac{2}{3}{I}_{2}{I}_{3}^{-5/3}\frac{\partial {I}_{3}}{\partial \boldsymbol{E}}∂E∂J2​​=I3−2/3​∂E∂I2​​−32​I2​I3−5/3​∂E∂I3​​
∂J3∂E=12I3−1/2∂I3∂E\frac{\partial {J}_{3}}{\partial \boldsymbol{E}}=\frac{1}{2}{I}_{3}^{-1/2}\frac{\partial {I}_{3}}{\partial \boldsymbol{E}}∂E∂J3​​=21​I3−1/2​∂E∂I3​​

For clarity, an equivalent compact expression uses only second-order tensors:

S=2A10J−2/3[I−I13C−1]  +2A01J−4/3[I1I−C−2I23C−1]  +KJ(J−1)C−1\boldsymbol{S}=2{A}_{10}{J}^{-2/3}\left[ \boldsymbol{I}-\frac{{I}_{1}}{3}{\boldsymbol{C}}^{-1}\right]\;+2{A}_{01}{J}^{-4/3}\left[ {I}_{1}\boldsymbol{I}-\boldsymbol{C}-\frac{2{I}_{2}}{3}{\boldsymbol{C}}^{-1}\right]\;+KJ\left( J-1\right){\boldsymbol{C}}^{-1}S=2A10​J−2/3[I−3I1​​C−1]+2A01​J−4/3[I1​I−C−32I2​​C−1]+KJ(J−1)C−1

The expression is nonlinear in deformation, unlike the familiar linear relationship between S\boldsymbol{S}S and E\boldsymbol{E}E for the Saint-Venant–Kirchhoff model. It remains hyperelastic because it is derived from the energy.

05

Consistent material linearization

Following [3], the consistent constitutive tangent is obtained by differentiating the Second Piola–Kirchhoff stress, as in Section 18.1, Section 18.2, Section 18.3.

The material constitutive tangent is defined as:

D=∂S∂E\boldsymbol D=\frac{\partial\boldsymbol S}{\partial\boldsymbol E}D=∂E∂S​

For the Mooney–Rivlin strain-energy function, its expanded expression is:

D=A10∂2J1∂E2+A01∂2J2∂E2  +K(J3−1)∂2J3∂E2  +K∂J3∂E(∂J3∂E)T\boldsymbol{D}={A}_{10}\frac{{\partial }^{2}{J}_{1}}{\partial {\boldsymbol{E}}^{2}}+{A}_{01}\frac{{\partial }^{2}{J}_{2}}{\partial {\boldsymbol{E}}^{2}}\;+K\left( {J}_{3}-1\right)\frac{{\partial }^{2}{J}_{3}}{\partial {\boldsymbol{E}}^{2}}\;+K\frac{\partial {J}_{3}}{\partial \boldsymbol{E}}{\left( \frac{\partial {J}_{3}}{\partial \boldsymbol{E}}\right)}^{T}D=A10​∂E2∂2J1​​+A01​∂E2∂2J2​​+K(J3​−1)∂E2∂2J3​​+K∂E∂J3​​(∂E∂J3​​)T

The material tangent D\boldsymbol DD is represented by a 6×66\times66×6 matrix. Its entries depend on the deformation, in contrast with the constant Saint-Venant–Kirchhoff material tangent. The component-level derivatives and matrices are given in Appendix A, together with the engineering-shear convention used for their differentiation.

07

Postprocessing: Hencky strain and Cauchy stress

For comparison with ANSYS Mechanical APDL, the Green–Lagrange strains and Second Piola–Kirchhoff stresses used in the present Total Lagrangian formulation are post-processed to obtain Hencky (logarithmic) strains and Cauchy (true) stresses, respectively. These are the standard strain and stress quantities reported by ANSYS for hyperelastic materials.

The TL formulation calculates Second Piola–Kirchhoff stress and Green–Lagrange strain with reference to the initial configuration. For comparison with other output measures, the results are converted to Cauchy stress and Hencky strain, as discussed in Chapter 14 and Section 17.1.

The right stretch tensor and Hencky logarithmic strain are:

U=FTF=C\boldsymbol{U}=\sqrt{{\boldsymbol{F}}^{T}\boldsymbol{F}}=\sqrt{\boldsymbol{C}}U=FTF​=C​
εH=ln⁡U{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}_{H}=\ln \boldsymbol{U}εH​=lnU

The following excerpt from convert_strain.m calculates the Hencky strain:

ux=Bux*sel; uy=Buy*sel; uz=Buz*sel;
vx=Bvx*sel; vy=Bvy*sel; vz=Bvz*sel; 
wx=Bwx*sel; wy=Bwy*sel; wz=Bwz*sel;

FF =[1+ux uy uz; vx 1+vy vz; wx wy 1+wz];
C=FF'*FF;
U=sqrtm(C);
ee=logm(U);      % Hencky strain tensor

The Cauchy stress follows from:

σ=1JFSFT,J=det⁡(F)\boldsymbol{\sigma}=\frac{1}{J}\boldsymbol F\boldsymbol S{\boldsymbol{F}}^{T},\quad \quad J=\det(\boldsymbol F)σ=J1​FSFT,J=det(F)

The corresponding excerpt from convert_stress.m is:

ux=Bux*sel; uy=Buy*sel; uz=Buz*sel;
vx=Bvx*sel; vy=Bvy*sel; vz=Bvz*sel; 
wx=Bwx*sel; wy=Bwy*sel; wz=Bwz*sel;

FF=[1+ux uy  uz; vx 1+vy  vz; wx  wy  1+wz];
% 2nd Piola-Kirchhoff to Cauchy stress
ja=det(FF);
sgm=sigmt(ig,:,istep+1);
sPK2=[sgm(1) sgm(4) sgm(6); sgm(4) sgm(2) sgm(5); sgm(6) sgm(5) sgm(3)];
scauchy=1/ja*FF*sPK2*FF';

Compressible hyperelastic materials

The Mooney–Rivlin model can also describe compressible hyperelastic materials by including a volumetric strain-energy term with a finite, positive bulk modulus KKK. For the nominal values EY=4 MPaE_Y=4\,\mathrm{\mathrm{MPa}}EY​=4MPa and ν=0.495\nu=0.495ν=0.495, the example uses K=133.33 MPaK=133.33\,\mathrm{\mathrm{MPa}}K=133.33MPa. Illustratively, EY=4 MPaE_Y=4\,\mathrm{\mathrm{MPa}}EY​=4MPa and ν=0.3\nu=0.3ν=0.3 would instead give K=3.33 MPaK=3.33\,\mathrm{\mathrm{MPa}}K=3.33MPa, a bulk modulus 40 times smaller. This comparison is illustrative only: consistent parameter identification must also account for the shear modulus defined by the Mooney–Rivlin constants. The parameters of the numerical example are unchanged. Porous elastomers and elastomeric foams may require dedicated hyperelastic models to represent their strong volume changes.

08

Conclusions

The Mooney–Rivlin model represents nonlinear, finite-strain, hyperelastic behaviour through a strain-energy function of invariants. Reduced invariants allow distortion and volume change to be treated separately. The nearly incompressible formulation is illustrated by the MATLAB–ANSYS comparison above.

The useful range of the model depends on material characterization and loading paths. Its scientific value here is the combination of constitutive theory, explicit consistent linearization, working MATLAB examples and comparison with established FEM results.

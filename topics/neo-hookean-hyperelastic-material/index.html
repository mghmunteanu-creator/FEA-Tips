01

Introduction

Section 31.3 presented the Mooney–Rivlin hyperelastic material, whose strain-energy density depends on the first two reduced invariants of the right Cauchy–Green deformation tensor.

The Neo-Hookean model is a particular case of the Mooney–Rivlin model, obtained by setting the coefficient of the second reduced invariant to zero:

A01=0,A10=G02A_{01}=0,\qquad A_{10}=\frac{G_0}{2}A01​=0,A10​=2G0​​

where G0G_0G0​ is the initial shear modulus.

This simplification produces one of the most widely used hyperelastic constitutive models. Although the Neo-Hookean model contains only one distortional material parameter, it can describe finite elastic deformations and represents a substantial improvement over the Saint-Venant–Kirchhoff model for many rubber-like materials [1, 2].

As in Section 31.3, the Total Lagrangian formulation is adopted. The strain-energy density is defined per unit initial volume, and the Second Piola–Kirchhoff stresses are energetically conjugate to the Green–Lagrange strains.

The present section emphasizes the constitutive specialization, its physical interpretation, and its numerical implementation using the MATLAB programs developed for the Mooney–Rivlin model.

02

Deformation measures and reduced invariants

The deformation measures and invariants were introduced in Section 31.3. Only the relations required for the present model are recalled here.

The deformation gradient and the right Cauchy–Green tensor are:

F=I+∂u∂x\boldsymbol F=\boldsymbol I+\frac{\partial\boldsymbol u}{\partial\boldsymbol x}F=I+∂x∂u​

Here, x\boldsymbol xx denotes the coordinates of the initial configuration, as required by the Total Lagrangian formulation.

C=FTF=2E+I\boldsymbol C=\boldsymbol F^T\boldsymbol F=2\boldsymbol E+\boldsymbol IC=FTF=2E+I

The first and third invariants are:

I1=tr⁡(C),I3=det⁡(C)I_1=\operatorname{tr}(\boldsymbol C),\qquad I_3=\det(\boldsymbol C)I1​=tr(C),I3​=det(C)

The local volume ratio is:

J=det⁡(F)=I31/2J=\det(\boldsymbol F)=I_3^{1/2}J=det(F)=I31/2​

For the Neo-Hookean model, only the first reduced invariant is needed:

Iˉ1=I1I3−1/3=J−2/3I1\bar I_1=I_1I_3^{-1/3}=J^{-2/3}I_1Iˉ1​=I1​I3−1/3​=J−2/3I1​

The reduced invariant separates shape changes from uniform volume changes. For example, under pure volumetric deformation:

F=λI\boldsymbol F=\lambda\boldsymbol IF=λI

the following relations hold:

C=λ2I,I1=3λ2,J=λ3\boldsymbol C=\lambda^2\boldsymbol I,\qquad I_1=3\lambda^2,\qquad J=\lambda^3C=λ2I,I1​=3λ2,J=λ3

Consequently:

Iˉ1=J−2/3I1=3\bar I_1=J^{-2/3}I_1=3Iˉ1​=J−2/3I1​=3

Thus, the reduced first invariant remains unchanged under pure volumetric deformation. This property allows the distortional and volumetric contributions to the strain energy to be treated separately.

03

Neo-Hookean strain-energy function

The Mooney–Rivlin strain-energy function from Section 31.3 is:

W=A10(Iˉ1−3)+A01(Iˉ2−3)+K2(J−1)2W=A_{10}(\bar I_1-3)+A_{01}(\bar I_2-3)+\frac K2(J-1)^2W=A10​(Iˉ1​−3)+A01​(Iˉ2​−3)+2K​(J−1)2

Setting A01A_{01}A01​ = 0 and A10A_{10}A10​ = G0G_0G0​/2 yields the Neo-Hookean energy:

W=G02(Iˉ1−3)+K2(J−1)2W=\frac{G_0}{2}(\bar I_1-3)+\frac K2(J-1)^2W=2G0​​(Iˉ1​−3)+2K​(J−1)2

The two contributions have different physical meanings:

W=Wdist+WvolW=W_{dist}+W_{vol}W=Wdist​+Wvol​

where:

Wdist=G02(Iˉ1−3)W_{dist}=\frac{G_0}{2}(\bar I_1-3)Wdist​=2G0​​(Iˉ1​−3)
Wvol=K2(J−1)2W_{vol}=\frac K2(J-1)^2Wvol​=2K​(J−1)2

The distortional contribution describes changes in shape, while the volumetric contribution penalizes changes in volume. The material parameters are the initial shear modulus G0G_0G0​ and the bulk modulus KKK.

In the small-strain limit, their relationship with the equivalent Young’s modulus and Poisson’s ratio is:

G0=E02(1+ν0)G_0=\frac{E_0}{2(1+\nu_0)}G0​=2(1+ν0​)E0​​
K=E03(1−2ν0)K=\frac{E_0}{3(1-2\nu_0)}K=3(1−2ν0​)E0​​

For nearly incompressible materials, ν0\nu_0ν0​ approaches 0.5 and KKK becomes much larger than G0G_0G0​.

The quadratic volumetric energy adopted here is the same as in Section 31.3. Other compressible Neo-Hookean formulations exist, including models with logarithmic volumetric terms. These alternatives are not considered in the present section.

04

Second Piola–Kirchhoff stress

The Second Piola–Kirchhoff stress follows from differentiation of the strain-energy density with respect to the Green–Lagrange strain [1, 2]:

S=∂W∂E=2∂W∂C\boldsymbol S=\frac{\partial W}{\partial\boldsymbol E}=2\frac{\partial W}{\partial\boldsymbol C}S=∂E∂W​=2∂C∂W​

Since:

∂I1∂C=I\frac{\partial I_1}{\partial\boldsymbol C}=\boldsymbol I∂C∂I1​​=I
∂J∂C=J2C−1\frac{\partial J}{\partial\boldsymbol C}=\frac J2\boldsymbol C^{-1}∂C∂J​=2J​C−1

the derivative of the reduced first invariant becomes:

∂Iˉ1∂C=J−2/3[I−I13C−1]\frac{\partial\bar I_1}{\partial\boldsymbol C}=J^{-2/3}\left[\boldsymbol I-\frac{I_1}{3}\boldsymbol C^{-1}\right]∂C∂Iˉ1​​=J−2/3[I−3I1​​C−1]

Differentiating the strain-energy function gives the constitutive relationship:

S=G0J−2/3[I−I13C−1]+KJ(J−1)C−1\boldsymbol S=G_0J^{-2/3}\left[\boldsymbol I-\frac{I_1}{3}\boldsymbol C^{-1}\right]+KJ(J-1)\boldsymbol C^{-1}S=G0​J−2/3[I−3I1​​C−1]+KJ(J−1)C−1

This expression is also obtained directly from the Mooney–Rivlin stress equation in Section 31.3 by setting A01A_{01}A01​ = 0 and 2A10A_{10}A10​ = G0G_0G0​.

Unlike the Saint-Venant–Kirchhoff model, the relationship between S\boldsymbol SS and E\boldsymbol EE is nonlinear.

Material constitutive tangent

The consistent material tangent is defined as:

D=∂S∂E\boldsymbol D=\frac{\partial\boldsymbol S}{\partial\boldsymbol E}D=∂E∂S​

Its expression follows directly from the Mooney–Rivlin tangent developed in Section 31.3 by setting A01A_{01}A01​ = 0 and A10A_{10}A10​ = G0G_0G0​/2 [3].

In the six-component engineering representation used by the MATLAB implementation, the specialised tangent is:

D=G02∂2Iˉ1∂E2+K(J−1)∂2J∂E2+K∂J∂E(∂J∂E)T\boldsymbol D=\frac{G_0}{2}\frac{\partial^2\bar I_1}{\partial\boldsymbol E^2}+K(J-1)\frac{\partial^2J}{\partial\boldsymbol E^2}+K\frac{\partial J}{\partial\boldsymbol E}\left(\frac{\partial J}{\partial\boldsymbol E}\right)^TD=2G0​​∂E2∂2Iˉ1​​+K(J−1)∂E2∂2J​+K∂E∂J​(∂E∂J​)T

In the six-component engineering representation, the last term involves the product of a 6 × 1 column vector and a 1 × 6 row vector, yielding a 6 × 6 matrix.

The first term follows from differentiating the reduced invariant:

∂2Iˉ1∂E2=−13I3−4/3[∂I1∂E(∂I3∂E)T+∂I3∂E(∂I1∂E)T]+49I1I3−7/3∂I3∂E(∂I3∂E)T−13I1I3−4/3∂2I3∂E2\begin{aligned}\frac{\partial^2\bar I_1}{\partial\boldsymbol E^2}={}&-\frac13I_3^{-4/3}\left[\frac{\partial I_1}{\partial\boldsymbol E}\left(\frac{\partial I_3}{\partial\boldsymbol E}\right)^T+\frac{\partial I_3}{\partial\boldsymbol E}\left(\frac{\partial I_1}{\partial\boldsymbol E}\right)^T\right]\\&+\frac49I_1I_3^{-7/3}\frac{\partial I_3}{\partial\boldsymbol E}\left(\frac{\partial I_3}{\partial\boldsymbol E}\right)^T-\frac13I_1I_3^{-4/3}\frac{\partial^2 I_3}{\partial\boldsymbol E^2}\end{aligned}∂E2∂2Iˉ1​​=​−31​I3−4/3​[∂E∂I1​​(∂E∂I3​​)T+∂E∂I3​​(∂E∂I1​​)T]+94​I1​I3−7/3​∂E∂I3​​(∂E∂I3​​)T−31​I1​I3−4/3​∂E2∂2I3​​​

The volumetric derivatives are:

∂J∂E=12I3−1/2∂I3∂E\frac{\partial J}{\partial\boldsymbol E}=\frac12 I_3^{-1/2}\frac{\partial I_3}{\partial\boldsymbol E}∂E∂J​=21​I3−1/2​∂E∂I3​​
∂2J∂E2=12I3−1/2∂2I3∂E2−14I3−3/2∂I3∂E(∂I3∂E)T\frac{\partial^2 J}{\partial\boldsymbol E^2}=\frac12 I_3^{-1/2}\frac{\partial^2 I_3}{\partial\boldsymbol E^2}-\frac14 I_3^{-3/2}\frac{\partial I_3}{\partial\boldsymbol E}\left(\frac{\partial I_3}{\partial\boldsymbol E}\right)^T∂E2∂2J​=21​I3−1/2​∂E2∂2I3​​−41​I3−3/2​∂E∂I3​​(∂E∂I3​​)T

Here, ∂2I1/∂E2=0\partial^2 I_1/\partial\boldsymbol E^2=\boldsymbol 0∂2I1​/∂E2=0. The invariant derivatives, the six-component ordering and the engineering-shear factors are those given in Section 31.3, Appendix A.

Consequently, no additional constitutive algorithm is required for the Neo-Hookean material. The same finite element formulation and Newton–Raphson solution procedure can be used.

05

Cauchy stress

The Cauchy stress tensor is obtained from the Second Piola–Kirchhoff stress through:

σ=1JFSFT\boldsymbol\sigma=\frac1J\boldsymbol F\boldsymbol S\boldsymbol F^Tσ=J1​FSFT

Introducing the left Cauchy–Green tensor:

b=FFT\boldsymbol b=\boldsymbol F\boldsymbol F^Tb=FFT

and its volume-corrected form:

bˉ=J−2/3b\bar{\boldsymbol b}=J^{-2/3}\boldsymbol bbˉ=J−2/3b

the Cauchy stress becomes:

σ=G0J[bˉ−13tr⁡(bˉ)I]+K(J−1)I\boldsymbol\sigma=\frac{G_0}{J}\left[\bar{\boldsymbol b}-\frac13\operatorname{tr}(\bar{\boldsymbol b})\boldsymbol I\right]+K(J-1)\boldsymbol Iσ=JG0​​[bˉ−31​tr(bˉ)I]+K(J−1)I

The first term is deviatoric and describes the distortional response. The second term is spherical and accounts for the volumetric response. This decomposition is particularly useful for interpreting the behavior of nearly incompressible materials.

06

Two simple deformation examples

Pure volumetric deformation

Consider the deformation:

F=λI\boldsymbol F=\lambda\boldsymbol IF=λI

As shown in Subsection 02, the reduced first invariant remains equal to 3:

Iˉ1=3\bar I_1=3Iˉ1​=3

The strain-energy density therefore reduces to:

W=K2(λ3−1)2W=\frac K2(\lambda^3-1)^2W=2K​(λ3−1)2

The resulting Cauchy stress is purely spherical:

σ=K(λ3−1)I\boldsymbol\sigma=K(\lambda^3-1)\boldsymbol Iσ=K(λ3−1)I

Thus, pure volumetric deformation produces no distortional energy contribution.

Simple shear

Consider a simple shear deformation:

F=[1γ0010001]\boldsymbol F=\begin{bmatrix}1&\gamma&0\\0&1&0\\0&0&1\end{bmatrix}F=​100​γ10​001​​

where γ\gammaγ is the engineering shear. For this deformation:

J=1,I1=3+γ2J=1,\qquad I_1=3+\gamma^2J=1,I1​=3+γ2

Therefore:

Iˉ1=3+γ2\bar I_1=3+\gamma^2Iˉ1​=3+γ2

The strain-energy density becomes:

W=G02γ2W=\frac{G_0}{2}\gamma^2W=2G0​​γ2

The Cauchy shear stress is:

σ12=G0γ\sigma_{12}=G_0\gammaσ12​=G0​γ

The model also predicts a normal-stress difference:

σ11−σ22=G0γ2\sigma_{11}-\sigma_{22}=G_0\gamma^2σ11​−σ22​=G0​γ2

Although the shear stress is linear in γ\gammaγ for this particular deformation, the finite-strain response includes normal-stress effects that are absent from classical infinitesimal-strain shear theory.

07

Numerical implementation and comparison with Mooney–Rivlin

An important advantage of treating Neo-Hookean elasticity as a particular case of Mooney–Rivlin elasticity is that the MATLAB programs developed in Section 31.3 can be reused without changing the finite element formulation.

Use the Mooney–Rivlin MATLAB program from Section 31.3, changing only the following constants in gen.m:

A10=0.59;             % Neo-Hookean constant
A01=0;                % Second invariant contribution

All terms proportional to A01A_{01}A01​ vanish automatically, including their contributions to the constitutive stress and tangent stiffness.

For a meaningful comparison between the two material models, the same geometry, boundary conditions, loading and initial elastic moduli should be used.

In the Mooney–Rivlin example of Section 31.3:

A10=0.48 MPa,A01=0.11 MPaA_{10}=0.48\,\mathrm{MPa},\qquad A_{01}=0.11\,\mathrm{MPa}A10​=0.48MPa,A01​=0.11MPa

Consequently:

G0=2(A10+A01)=1.18 MPaG_0=2(A_{10}+A_{01})=1.18\,\mathrm{MPa}G0​=2(A10​+A01​)=1.18MPa

The corresponding Neo-Hookean parameters are:

A10=0.59 MPa,A01=0A_{10}=0.59\,\mathrm{MPa},\qquad A_{01}=0A10​=0.59MPa,A01​=0

Both models can therefore be compared using the same initial shear modulus, G0G_0G0​ = 1.18 MPa, and bulk modulus, KKK = 133.33 MPa.

In the program, K=4/[3(1−2×0.495)]=133.333… MPaK=4/[3(1-2\times0.495)]=133.333\ldots\,\mathrm{MPa}K=4/[3(1−2×0.495)]=133.333…MPa. The nominal values used to specify this bulk modulus are kept separate from the independently prescribed G0=1.18 MPaG_0=1.18\,\mathrm{MPa}G0​=1.18MPa, as in Section 31.3.

The difference results from the different dependence of the distortional strain energy on the deformation invariants. 08

Applicability and limitations

The Neo-Hookean model is one of the simplest constitutive models suitable for finite elastic deformations [1, 2].

It is particularly useful when the material behavior can be represented adequately by a single distortional parameter and a separate volumetric contribution.

However, the Neo-Hookean model does not reproduce all experimentally observed nonlinear responses of rubber-like materials.

Compared with the two-parameter Mooney–Rivlin model, it provides less flexibility in describing different deformation modes.

The Neo-Hookean model is therefore attractive for preliminary analyses, numerical verification and materials whose measured response is adequately represented by this constitutive law.

Its suitability for a particular application should be assessed against experimental data, especially when large strains or several loading modes are involved.

The comparison with Section 31.3 illustrates how the two models can predict different finite-deformation responses even when their initial elastic moduli are identical.

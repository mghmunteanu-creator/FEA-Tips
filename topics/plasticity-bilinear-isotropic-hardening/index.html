Hardening rules

StrainSmall
DisplacementLarge
MaterialElastoplastic

The direct and consistent Updated Lagrangian formulation of Section 27.2 is used. Plasticity theory describes the elastoplastic response through three ingredients: the yield criterion, the flow rule and the hardening rule [1]. The first two were introduced in Section 30.2; here we consider the third.

The hardening rule describes how the yield surface changes as plastic deformation develops. With isotropic hardening, the surface remains centred at the same point and expands. With kinematic hardening, its size remains constant while its centre translates [1].

Only rate-independent, bilinear isotropic hardening is considered below. Plastic strain develops when the yield condition is reached, without an explicit dependence on time in the adopted material law. The uniaxial response has an initial elastic slope EEE and a post-yield tangent modulus EtE_tEt​:

Unloading is elastic, with slope EEE, parallel to the initial elastic branch. Plastic deformation increases the yield stress from its initial value σ0(0)\sigma_0(0)σ0​(0) to the current value σ0(κ)\sigma_0(\kappa)σ0​(κ):

03

Tangent modulus and plastic modulus

For uniaxial loading beyond the elastic limit, the stress increment is related to the total strain increment by the tangent modulus [2]:

dσ=Et dε=Et(dεel+dεpl) d\sigma=E_t\,d\varepsilon=E_t(d\varepsilon^{el}+d\varepsilon^{pl}) dσ=Et​dε=Et​(dεel+dεpl)

Using Hooke’s law for the elastic part gives:

dσ=E dεel,dσ=Et(dσE+dεpl),dσEt−dσE=dεpl d\sigma=E\,d\varepsilon^{el},\qquad d\sigma=E_t\left(\frac{d\sigma}{E}+d\varepsilon^{pl}\right),\qquad \frac{d\sigma}{E_t}-\frac{d\sigma}{E}=d\varepsilon^{pl} dσ=Edεel,dσ=Et​(Edσ​+dεpl),Et​dσ​−Edσ​=dεpl

Therefore, for monotonic uniaxial plastic loading:

dσ=H dεpl,H=Et1−Et/E=EEtE−Et d\sigma=H\,d\varepsilon^{pl},\qquad H=\frac{E_t}{1-E_t/E}=\frac{EE_t}{E-E_t} dσ=Hdεpl,H=1−Et​/EEt​​=E−Et​EEt​​

The plastic modulus HHH is the slope of yield stress versus accumulated equivalent plastic strain, not the slope EtE_tEt​ of stress versus total strain. When Et≪EE_t\ll EEt​≪E:

H≃Et H\simeq E_t H≃Et​

Seven-equation local nonlinear system

Let σn\boldsymbol\sigma_nσn​ and κn\kappa_nκn​ denote the previous converged state. Using the gradient at the beginning of a finite increment would give:

Δσ=DΔε−ΔλD∇F(σn) \Delta\boldsymbol\sigma=\boldsymbol D\Delta\boldsymbol\varepsilon-\Delta\lambda\boldsymbol D\nabla F(\boldsymbol\sigma_n) Δσ=DΔε−ΔλD∇F(σn​)

As explained in Section 30.2, this does not generally return the stress to the yield surface. The implicit update uses the gradient at the corrected stress:

Δσ=DΔε−ΔλD∇F(σn+Δσ) \Delta\boldsymbol\sigma=\boldsymbol D\Delta\boldsymbol\varepsilon-\Delta\lambda\boldsymbol D\nabla F(\boldsymbol\sigma_n+\Delta\boldsymbol\sigma) Δσ=DΔε−ΔλD∇F(σn​+Δσ)

Example 1. Uniform tensile loading

A rectangular bar of length 200 mm and cross-section 50×6 mm50\times6\,\mathrm{mm}50×6mm is subjected to a uniformly distributed axial force of 13,000 N. Its material properties are:

E=1000 MPa,Et=100 MPa,ν=0.3,σ0(0)=40 MPa E=1000\,\mathrm{MPa},\qquad E_t=100\,\mathrm{MPa},\qquad \nu=0.3,\qquad \sigma_0(0)=40\,\mathrm{MPa} E=1000MPa,Et​=100MPa,ν=0.3,σ0​(0)=40MPa

The corresponding plastic modulus is H=111.11 MPaH=111.11\,\mathrm{MPa}H=111.11MPa. The supported end permits transverse contraction. The mesh has 18 nodes and four HEX8 elements, and the bar is subjected to uniform tension.

Simplified beam-theory comparison

For a prescribed tensile stress σ\sigmaσ, the elastic axial strain is:

εxel=σE \varepsilon_x^{el}=\frac{\sigma}{E} εxel​=Eσ​

Above the initial yield stress, the plastic axial strain is:

εxpl=σ−σ0(0)H \varepsilon_x^{pl}=\frac{\sigma-\sigma_0(0)}{H} εxpl​=Hσ−σ0​(0)​

The magnitudes of the elastic transverse contractions are:

∣εyel∣=∣εzel∣=νεxel=νσE |\varepsilon_y^{el}|=|\varepsilon_z^{el}|=\nu\varepsilon_x^{el}=\nu\frac{\sigma}{E} ∣εyel​∣=∣εzel​∣=νεxel​=νEσ​

The magnitudes of the plastic transverse contractions are:

∣εypl∣=∣εzpl∣=12εxpl=σ−σ0(0)2H |\varepsilon_y^{pl}|=|\varepsilon_z^{pl}|=\frac12\varepsilon_x^{pl}=\frac{\sigma-\sigma_0(0)}{2H} ∣εypl​∣=∣εzpl​∣=21​εxpl​=2Hσ−σ0​(0)​

Taking the reduction in cross-sectional area into account, the tensile force is:

F(σ)=σA(1−νεxel−εxpl2)2=σA(1−νσE−σ−σ0(0)2H)2 F(\sigma)=\sigma A\left(1-\nu\varepsilon_x^{el}-\frac{\varepsilon_x^{pl}}2\right)^2=\sigma A\left(1-\nu\frac{\sigma}{E}-\frac{\sigma-\sigma_0(0)}{2H}\right)^2 F(σ)=σA(1−νεxel​−2εxpl​​)2=σA(1−νEσ​−2Hσ−σ0​(0)​)2

The elongation is:

Δl(σ)=(εxel+εxpl)L=(σE+σ−σ0(0)H)L \Delta l(\sigma)=(\varepsilon_x^{el}+\varepsilon_x^{pl})L=\left(\frac{\sigma}{E}+\frac{\sigma-\sigma_0(0)}{H}\right)L Δl(σ)=(εxel​+εxpl​)L=(Eσ​+Hσ−σ0​(0)​)L

The expressions involving (σ−σ0(0))/H(\sigma-\sigma_0(0))/H(σ−σ0​(0))/H apply after yielding; the plastic strain is zero in the elastic range. The short MATLAB program below evaluates this comparison:

L=200;
A=6*50;
sgy=40;
E=1000;
Et=100;
H=Et/(1-Et/E);     % Plastic modulus
sg_max=48;
for s=1:sg_max
    ee=s/E;
    epl=0;
    if s>sgy
        epl=(s-sgy)/H;
    end
    Force(s+1)=s*A*(1-0.3*ee-0.5*epl)^2;
    Dl(s+1)=(ee+epl)*L;
end

Example 2. Perforated plate

The perforated plate has height 140 mm, width 100 mm, hole diameter 54 mm and thickness 2 mm. The mesh contains 1534 nodes and 685 HEX8 elements, with one element through the thickness. The total applied tensile force is F=5000 NF=5000\,\mathrm NF=5000N. The material properties are:

E=1000 MPa,Et=100 MPa,ν=0.3,σ0(0)=40 MPa E=1000\,\mathrm{MPa},\qquad E_t=100\,\mathrm{MPa},\qquad \nu=0.3,\qquad \sigma_0(0)=40\,\mathrm{MPa} E=1000MPa,Et​=100MPa,ν=0.3,σ0​(0)=40MPa

The MATLAB–ANSYS comparison is shown below. The MATLAB curve uses 100 load steps, with load factor 1 corresponding to the total force of 5000 N.

The MATLAB stress maps and the plastic-zone animation use 100 load steps at 5000 N. The maximum absolute vertical displacement is 24.4838 mm. The nodally averaged von Mises stress ranges from 4.31512 to 74.5628 MPa.

At the maximum applied load of 5000 N, the plate undergoes extremely large deformations, with strains that exceed the range of validity of the small-strain assumptions adopted in the constitutive formulation. Large displacements alone do not imply large strains; in this example, both occur.

This deliberately demanding example is intended primarily to illustrate the development and propagation of plastic zones, the redistribution of stresses, the influence of isotropic hardening, and the numerical behavior of the Updated Lagrangian solution procedure under severe geometric nonlinearity.

The MATLAB results show very close agreement with those obtained using ANSYS for the corresponding numerical analyses. Nevertheless, agreement between numerical implementations does not remove the limitations of the adopted constitutive assumptions. The example should therefore be regarded as an illustration of the computational formulation rather than a physically validated prediction at such extreme strains.

The animation shows plastic zones developing while the plate deforms. Gauss-point plastic indicators are averaged at the nodes as in plot_plast.m, allowing partial plasticization within an element. Black lines show the mesh contours; the deformation scale is 1.

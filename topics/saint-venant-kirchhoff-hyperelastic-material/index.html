01

Introduction: a simple model with an important distinction

In Section 31.1, we introduced the energetic conjugacy of the Green–Lagrange strain tensor E\boldsymbol{E}E and the Second Piola–Kirchhoff stress tensor S\boldsymbol{S}S. For an infinitesimal additional deformation, the change in internal work per unit initial volume is:

dW0=S:dEd{W}_{0}=\boldsymbol{S}:d\boldsymbol{E}dW0​=S:dE

The two symmetric tensors can be written explicitly as:

S=[SxxSxySxzSxySyySyzSxzSyzSzz]\boldsymbol S=\begin{bmatrix}S_{xx}&S_{xy}&S_{xz}\\S_{xy}&S_{yy}&S_{yz}\\S_{xz}&S_{yz}&S_{zz}\end{bmatrix}S=​Sxx​Sxy​Sxz​​Sxy​Syy​Syz​​Sxz​Syz​Szz​​​
E=[ExxExyExzExyEyyEyzExzEyzEzz]\boldsymbol E=\begin{bmatrix}E_{xx}&E_{xy}&E_{xz}\\E_{xy}&E_{yy}&E_{yz}\\E_{xz}&E_{yz}&E_{zz}\end{bmatrix}E=​Exx​Exy​Exz​​Exy​Eyy​Eyz​​Exz​Eyz​Ezz​​​

A hyperelastic material is characterized by a stored strain-energy function. When that function is expressed in terms of E\boldsymbol{E}E, the corresponding stress follows by differentiation:

S=∂W0∂E\boldsymbol{S}=\frac{\partial {W}_{0}}{\partial \boldsymbol{E}}S=∂E∂W0​​

The Saint-Venant–Kirchhoff (SV-K) model is one of the simplest possible illustrations. Its strain-energy function is quadratic in Green–Lagrange strain. The resulting relation between S\boldsymbol{S}S and E\boldsymbol{E}E has precisely the familiar linear isotropic form of Hooke’s law [1], [2].

Although the constitutive relationship between Second Piola–Kirchhoff stress and Green–Lagrange strain is linear, the overall response may be geometrically nonlinear. Moreover, the hyperelastic nature of the model does not guarantee its validity for large elastic strains.

02

Strain-energy function

For an isotropic Saint-Venant–Kirchhoff material, the stored energy per unit initial volume is:

W0(E)=λL2(tr⁡E)2+G E:E{W}_{0}\left( \boldsymbol{E}\right)=\frac{{\lambda }_{L}}{2}{\left( \operatorname{tr}\boldsymbol{E}\right)}^{2}+G\,\boldsymbol{E}:\boldsymbol{E}W0​(E)=2λL​​(trE)2+GE:E

Here tr⁡E=Exx+Eyy+Ezz\operatorname{tr}\boldsymbol{E}={E}_{xx}+{E}_{yy}+{E}_{zz}trE=Exx​+Eyy​+Ezz​, while λL{\lambda }_{L}λL​ and GGG are the Lamé constants. They are related to Young’s modulus EY{E}_{Y}EY​ and Poisson’s ratio ν\nu ν by:

λL=EYν(1+ν)(1−2ν)G=EY2(1+ν){\lambda }_{L}=\frac{{E}_{Y}\nu }{\left( 1+\nu \right)\left( 1-2\nu \right)}\quad \quad G=\frac{{E}_{Y}}{2\left( 1+\nu \right)}λL​=(1+ν)(1−2ν)EY​ν​G=2(1+ν)EY​​

We use λL{\lambda }_{L}λL​ rather than λ\lambda λ because a stretch ratio will appear later. The subscript 000 in W0{W}_{0}W0​ indicates energy per unit initial volume, as in Section 31.1; it does not denote an initial energy value. The energy is assumed to vanish in the undeformed, stress-free state E=0\boldsymbol{E}=\boldsymbol{0}E=0.

The energy depends on strain, rather than on the path by which that strain was reached. This is the defining hyperelastic feature of the model [1], [2].

03

Deriving the Second Piola–Kirchhoff stress

Differentiating the energy gives:

dW0=λL(tr⁡E)tr⁡(dE)+2G E:dEd{W}_{0}={\lambda }_{L}\left( \operatorname{tr}\boldsymbol{E}\right)\operatorname{tr}\left( d\boldsymbol{E}\right)+2G\,\boldsymbol{E}:d\boldsymbol{E}dW0​=λL​(trE)tr(dE)+2GE:dE

Because tr⁡(dE)=I:dE\operatorname{tr}\left( d\boldsymbol{E}\right)=\boldsymbol{I}:d\boldsymbol{E}tr(dE)=I:dE, we can rearrange this as:

dW0=[λL(tr⁡E)I+2GE]:dEd{W}_{0}=\left[ {\lambda }_{L}\left( \operatorname{tr}\boldsymbol{E}\right)\boldsymbol{I}+2G\boldsymbol{E}\right]:d\boldsymbol{E}dW0​=[λL​(trE)I+2GE]:dE

Comparing with dW0=S:dEd{W}_{0}=\boldsymbol{S}:d\boldsymbol{E}dW0​=S:dE, we obtain the constitutive law:

S=λL(tr⁡E)I+2GE\boldsymbol{S}={\lambda }_{L}\left( \operatorname{tr}\boldsymbol{E}\right)\boldsymbol{I}+2G\boldsymbol{E}S=λL​(trE)I+2GE

This formula looks exactly like Hooke’s law for an isotropic linear elastic solid. The difference is that it relates Green–Lagrange strain to Second Piola–Kirchhoff stress, rather than infinitesimal strain to Cauchy stress.

For sufficiently small strains and rotations, E\boldsymbol{E}E approaches the infinitesimal strain tensor ε\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}ε, and S\boldsymbol{S}S approaches the Cauchy stress σ\boldsymbol{\sigma}σ. The classical small-strain constitutive relation is then recovered.

The model is therefore often described as materially linear but geometrically nonlinear.

04

Cauchy stress and the role of rigid-body rotation

The Green–Lagrange strain is obtained from the deformation gradient (see Chapter 10):

E=12(FTF−I)\boldsymbol{E}=\frac{1}{2}\left( {\boldsymbol{F}}^{T}\boldsymbol{F}-\boldsymbol{I}\right)E=21​(FTF−I)

The Cauchy stress in the current configuration is (see Chapter 14):

σ=1JFSFT\boldsymbol{\sigma}=\frac{1}{J}\boldsymbol F\boldsymbol S{\boldsymbol{F}}^{T}σ=J1​FSFT

Here J=det⁡(F)>0J=\det(\boldsymbol F)>0J=det(F)>0 is the current-to-initial volume ratio. Even though S\boldsymbol{S}S depends linearly on E\boldsymbol{E}E, σ\boldsymbol{\sigma}σ is not generally a linear function of the stretch.

Recall from Section 17.1 the polar decomposition:

F=RU\boldsymbol{F}=\boldsymbol R\boldsymbol UF=RU

where RTR=I{\boldsymbol{R}}^{T}\boldsymbol{R}=\boldsymbol{I}RTR=I and U\boldsymbol{U}U is the right stretch tensor. It follows that:

E=12(U2−I)\boldsymbol{E}=\frac{1}{2}\left( {\boldsymbol{U}}^{2}-\boldsymbol{I}\right)E=21​(U2−I)

The rotation disappears from E\boldsymbol{E}E and therefore from W0{W}_{0}W0​. A superimposed rigid rotation changes the orientation of the Cauchy stress components, but not the stored deformation energy.

Thus, large rigid-body rotations are not, by themselves, a limitation of Saint-Venant–Kirchhoff. The difficulty is the prediction of material response when the elastic strains themselves become large.

05

A homogeneous longitudinal stretch

To make the distinction between the stress measures explicit, consider the simple homogeneous deformation:

06

Applicability: how large may the strain become?

The Saint-Venant–Kirchhoff model is generally suitable for small elastic strains, but may exhibit unrealistic behavior at large deformations [1], [2].

There is no universal strain percentage at which the Saint-Venant–Kirchhoff model suddenly ceases to be valid. The acceptable range depends on the real material, loading mode, stress state, and accuracy required. Experimental evidence or comparison with a better-suited constitutive model is the relevant criterion [1], [2].

It is useful to distinguish two quantities. The longitudinal engineering strain is εeng=λ−1{\varepsilon }_{eng}=\lambda -1εeng​=λ−1, while the Green–Lagrange strain is EGL=(λ2−1)/2{E}_{GL}=\left( {\lambda }^{2}-1\right)/2EGL​=(λ2−1)/2. The following illustrative percentages refer to engineering strain, not to EGL{E}_{GL}EGL​.

  • 1–2%: Small-strain range; SV-K may be reasonable if the material remains linearly elastic.
  • About 5%: Check the material law and the desired accuracy; do not assume validity automatically.
  • About 10%: Comparison with measured data or another validated constitutive model becomes particularly important.
  • 20% or more: For most large-strain elastic applications, a more suitable hyperelastic model is usually preferable.

These percentages are illustrative engineering recommendations, not experimentally established or universal validity thresholds. For some materials, the linear elastic assumption fails well before any of these levels; for others, a restricted deformation path may give reasonable results over a wider interval.

Large displacement is not the same as large strain. A slender beam may undergo very large displacement and rotation while its local elastic strains remain small. A geometrically nonlinear TL formulation with SV-K can be suitable in such a case, even though the displacements are not small.

The Saint-Venant–Kirchhoff model can exhibit physically unrealistic behavior under severe compression: beyond a certain compression level, the magnitude of the nominal compressive stress decreases as compression increases further. This is a limitation of the constitutive model, not of the finite element formulation.

07

We had already implemented Saint-Venant–Kirchhoff!

A small triumph for FEA Tips: the Saint-Venant–Kirchhoff model was already present in our earlier Total Lagrangian formulations!

The two-dimensional plane-stress elements in Section 12.1, Section 12.2, Section 12.3 and the three-dimensional HEX8 and HEX20 elements in Section 26.1 and Section 26.2 employ Green–Lagrange strain and a linear isotropic law for the energetically conjugate Second Piola–Kirchhoff stress. That is precisely the SV-K material law. The 2D plane-stress formulation uses the appropriate reduced constitutive law.

Chapter 14 even introduces this model explicitly when discussing the different stress measures. What is new here is the energetic interpretation: we start from the stored energy and derive the stress rather than merely postulating a linear relation between S\boldsymbol{S}S and E\boldsymbol{E}E.

In other words, our earlier TL finite element codes had already been working with a hyperelastic constitutive model. We simply have more reason to celebrate it now!

A separate MATLAB solver is therefore unnecessary in this section. Readers interested in complete numerical implementations can consult the 2D plane-stress formulations in Section 12.1, Section 12.2, Section 12.3 and the 3D formulations in Section 26.1 and Section 26.2.

08

Concluding remarks

The Saint-Venant–Kirchhoff model shows that hyperelasticity is defined by the existence of a strain-energy function, not by the ability to reproduce arbitrary large elastic strains. Its simplicity is its main pedagogical advantage:

W0(E)=λL2(tr⁡E)2+GE:E{W}_{0}\left( \boldsymbol{E}\right)=\frac{{\lambda }_{L}}{2}{\left( \operatorname{tr}\boldsymbol{E}\right)}^{2}+G\boldsymbol{E}:\boldsymbol{E}W0​(E)=2λL​​(trE)2+GE:E

It follows that S=λL(tr⁡E)I+2GE\boldsymbol{S}={\lambda }_{L}\left( \operatorname{tr}\boldsymbol{E}\right)\boldsymbol{I}+2G\boldsymbol{E}S=λL​(trE)I+2GE. The model is an excellent bridge between the familiar linear elastic law and nonlinear constitutive theories. However, its compression behavior and its limited ability to represent large elastic stretches explain why other strain-energy functions are needed.

The next section introduces the Mooney–Rivlin model, one of the most widely used hyperelastic constitutive models, particularly for rubber-like and nearly incompressible materials.

Unlike Saint-Venant–Kirchhoff, the Mooney–Rivlin model is specifically intended to describe finite elastic deformations. Its strain-energy function, expressed in terms of deformation invariants, provides a more flexible representation of nonlinear elastic behavior.

In Section 31.4, we shall show that the simpler Neo-Hookean model is a particular case of the Mooney–Rivlin formulation.

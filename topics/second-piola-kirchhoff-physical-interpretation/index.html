01

Interpreting the stress measures

The second Piola–Kirchhoff stress tensor S\boldsymbol{S}S is extensively used in finite-deformation mechanics, especially in Total Lagrangian formulations, but its physical interpretation requires care.

The Cauchy stress tensor σ\boldsymbol{\sigma}σ has the most direct physical interpretation: it represents the actual traction on a surface of the current configuration. The first Piola–Kirchhoff stress tensor P\boldsymbol{P}P relates forces in the current configuration to areas defined in the initial configuration.

Let WWW denote the strain-energy density, i.e. the strain energy per unit volume of the initial configuration.

The Green–Lagrange strain E\boldsymbol{E}E and the second Piola–Kirchhoff stress S\boldsymbol{S}S form a conjugate strain–stress pair. In engineering notation, the variation of the strain-energy density may be written as:

δW=ST δE\boxed{\delta W=\boldsymbol{S}^{T}\,\delta\boldsymbol{E}}δW=STδE​

Here, the engineering-component vectors are:

E={ExEyγxy}S={SxSySxy}\boldsymbol{E}=\begin{Bmatrix}E_x\\E_y\\\gamma_{xy}\end{Bmatrix}\qquad\boldsymbol{S}=\begin{Bmatrix}S_x\\S_y\\S_{xy}\end{Bmatrix}E=⎩⎨⎧​Ex​Ey​γxy​​⎭⎬⎫​S=⎩⎨⎧​Sx​Sy​Sxy​​⎭⎬⎫​

This relation expresses the conjugate character of the Green–Lagrange strain E\boldsymbol{E}E and the second Piola–Kirchhoff stress S\boldsymbol{S}S.

For the linear constitutive law used on this site:

S=DE\boxed{\boldsymbol{S}=\boldsymbol{D}\boldsymbol{E}}S=DE​

where D\boldsymbol{D}D is the constitutive matrix of Hooke’s law.

In one dimension:

Sx=E Ex\boxed{S_x=E\,E_x}Sx​=EEx​​

This association makes PK2 very convenient in the Total Lagrangian formulation. However, S\boldsymbol{S}S should not be interpreted as the physical traction acting directly on a current surface.

03

From PK2 to the applied force

At finite deformation, it is not correct to identify the applied force directly with the initial area multiplied by PK2 stress:

F=A0SF=A_0SF=A0​S

The force referred to the initial area is instead related to the first Piola–Kirchhoff stress:

F=A0PF=A_0PF=A0​P

The two Piola–Kirchhoff stress measures satisfy:

P=FS\boxed{\boldsymbol{P}=\boldsymbol{F}\boldsymbol{S}}P=FS​

Here, bold F\boldsymbol{F}F denotes the deformation gradient, while the non-bold FFF used in the numerical example denotes the applied force. Recall:

F=I+∇u\boldsymbol{F}=\boldsymbol{I}+\nabla\boldsymbol{u}F=I+∇u

In one dimension, the deformation gradient reduces to the stretch ratio:

F⟶λ\boldsymbol{F}\longrightarrow\lambdaF⟶λ

Therefore:

P=λS\boxed{P=\lambda S}P=λS​

is the one-dimensional counterpart of P=FS\boldsymbol{P}=\boldsymbol{F}\boldsymbol{S}P=FS. Consequently:

F=A0λS\boxed{F=A_0\lambda S}F=A0​λS​

and:

F=EA0λEGL\boxed{F=EA_0\lambda E_{GL}}F=EA0​λEGL​​

05

The incorrect shortcut

If PK2 were incorrectly used directly in F=A0SF=A_0SF=A0​S, the equation would be:

0.08=x+12x20.08=x+\frac12x^20.08=x+21​x2

This would give approximately:

x≈0.077Δ≈30.8 mmx\approx0.077\qquad\Delta\approx30.8\ \mathrm{mm}x≈0.077Δ≈30.8 mm

The difference between 30.8 mm and 28.8 mm is explained by the missing stretch factor:

λ=1+ΔL0\boxed{\lambda=1+\frac{\Delta}{L_0}}λ=1+L0​Δ​​

which converts PK2 into PK1 in the one-dimensional relation:

P=λSP=\lambda SP=λS

06

Final remark

The second Piola–Kirchhoff stress has a precise and important mechanical role, but it is not the physical traction measure acting directly on a surface of the current configuration. Its usefulness in the Total Lagrangian formulation comes from its natural association with the Green–Lagrange strain tensor.

In one dimension, the conversion is:

S  →×λ  P  →×A0  F\boxed{S\;\xrightarrow{\times\lambda}\;P\;\xrightarrow{\times A_0}\;F}S×λ​P×A0​​F​

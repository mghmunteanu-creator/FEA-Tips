01

Tait-Bryan angles

Tait-Bryan angles are a particular three-angle parametrization of rotations, closely related to Euler angles [1]. They are utilised in aerospace applications to depict the aircraft's orientation in relation to the 3D world frame. In this site, the Tait-Bryan angles are utilised to define the position of a 3D beam, or only of an infinitely small segment dsdsds of a beam, in the global 3D reference frame.

The local reference frame of a beam, or of an infinitely short segment of a curved beam, is defined like this: xˉ\bar xxˉ-axis is the locus of the centres of gravity of its cross-sections, yˉ\bar yyˉ​-axis and zˉ\bar zzˉ-axis are the principal axes of its cross-section. The Tait-Bryan angles are presented in the figures below, [1].

We assume that the local reference frame is obtained as follows: initially the local frame coincides with the global one: the beam lies on the global xxx-axis and global yyy-axis and zzz-axis are parallel to the principal axes of its cross-sections. We apply three consecutive rotations. On this site, we prefer the order yaw, pitch and roll (called zy′x′′zy'x''zy′x′′):

  • a rotation of angle ψ\psiψ around the global zzz axis, → “yaw”
  • a rotation of angle θ\thetaθ around the new yyy axis (denoted y′y'y′), → “pitch”
  • finally a rotation with φ\varphiφ angle around the newest xxx axis (let's call it x′′x''x′′). → “roll”

In this section, R\boldsymbol RR is used as the passive transformation matrix from global components to local components. Positive rotation angles follow the right-hand rule and are defined successively, starting from the global reference frame and then with respect to the rotated reference frames.

03

Two Tait-Bryan solutions

If we know the Tait-Bryan angles it is very easy to find the rotation matrix R\boldsymbol RR. But computing the angles from the rotation matrix R\boldsymbol RR is more difficult. It will now be shown how this operation can be performed, [2].

First, the angle θ\thetaθ can be easily found. In fact, there are two solutions:

θ1=sin⁡−1(−R(1,3))andθ2=π−θ1\theta_1=\sin^{-1}\left(-R(1,3)\right)\qquad\text{and}\qquad\theta_2=\pi-\theta_1θ1​=sin−1(−R(1,3))andθ2​=π−θ1​

Then the angle ψ\psiψ can be found out by noting that:

tan⁡ψ=R(1,2)R(1,1)\tan\psi=\frac{R(1,2)}{R(1,1)}tanψ=R(1,1)R(1,2)​

Or better, using the Matlab command atan2:

ψ=atan2⁡(R(1,2),R(1,1))\psi=\operatorname{atan2}\left(R(1,2),R(1,1)\right)ψ=atan2(R(1,2),R(1,1))

atan2 is a four-quadrant inverse tangent, that is the angle ψ\psiψ results in the interval [−π,π][-\pi,\pi][−π,π]. Here we have to pay attention to the sign of cos⁡θ\cos\thetacosθ: when cos⁡θ>0\cos\theta>0cosθ>0 the angle ψ\psiψ is as shown above, but if cos⁡θ<0\cos\theta<0cosθ<0:

ψ=atan2⁡(−R(1,2),−R(1,1))\psi=\operatorname{atan2}\left(-R(1,2),-R(1,1)\right)ψ=atan2(−R(1,2),−R(1,1))

This issue is easily solved by writing this line like this:

ψ1,2=atan2⁡(R(1,2)cos⁡θ1,2,R(1,1)cos⁡θ1,2)\psi_{1,2}=\operatorname{atan2}\left(\frac{R(1,2)}{\cos\theta_{1,2}},\frac{R(1,1)}{\cos\theta_{1,2}}\right)ψ1,2​=atan2(cosθ1,2​R(1,2)​,cosθ1,2​R(1,1)​)

In a similar manner, we can calculate the angle φ\varphiφ:

φ1,2=atan2⁡(R(2,3)cos⁡θ1,2,R(3,3)cos⁡θ1,2)\varphi_{1,2}=\operatorname{atan2}\left(\frac{R(2,3)}{\cos\theta_{1,2}},\frac{R(3,3)}{\cos\theta_{1,2}}\right)φ1,2​=atan2(cosθ1,2​R(2,3)​,cosθ1,2​R(3,3)​)

In conclusion, in general, we get two different solutions: (ψ1θ1φ1)(\psi_1\quad\theta_1\quad\varphi_1)(ψ1​θ1​φ1​) and (ψ2θ2φ2)(\psi_2\quad\theta_2\quad\varphi_2)(ψ2​θ2​φ2​). Both are valid!

04

Gimbal lock

The special case cos⁡θ=0\cos\theta=0cosθ=0, corresponding to θ=±π/2\theta=\pm\pi/2θ=±π/2, is known as gimbal lock. In this configuration, two rotation axes become aligned, so yaw and roll can no longer be determined independently.

In the particular case cos⁡θ=0\cos\theta=0cosθ=0 there are two possibilities: θ=π/2\theta=\pi/2θ=π/2 or θ=−π/2\theta=-\pi/2θ=−π/2.

When θ=π/2\theta=\pi/2θ=π/2 the rotation matrix R\boldsymbol RR becomes:

R=[00−1−sin⁡(ψ−φ)cos⁡(ψ−φ)0cos⁡(ψ−φ)sin⁡(ψ−φ)0]\boldsymbol R=\begin{bmatrix}0&0&-1\\-\sin(\psi-\varphi)&\cos(\psi-\varphi)&0\\\cos(\psi-\varphi)&\sin(\psi-\varphi)&0\end{bmatrix}R=​0−sin(ψ−φ)cos(ψ−φ)​0cos(ψ−φ)sin(ψ−φ)​−100​​

In this case, it results:

ψ−φ=atan2⁡(R(3,2),R(3,1))\psi-\varphi=\operatorname{atan2}\left(R(3,2),R(3,1)\right)ψ−φ=atan2(R(3,2),R(3,1))

The angle φ\varphiφ can have any value. We prefer to choose φ=0\varphi=0φ=0 and the angle ψ\psiψ will thus result.

When θ=−π/2\theta=-\pi/2θ=−π/2 the rotation matrix R\boldsymbol RR becomes:

R=[001−sin⁡(ψ+φ)cos⁡(ψ+φ)0−cos⁡(ψ+φ)−sin⁡(ψ+φ)0]\boldsymbol R=\begin{bmatrix}0&0&1\\-\sin(\psi+\varphi)&\cos(\psi+\varphi)&0\\-\cos(\psi+\varphi)&-\sin(\psi+\varphi)&0\end{bmatrix}R=​0−sin(ψ+φ)−cos(ψ+φ)​0cos(ψ+φ)−sin(ψ+φ)​100​​

and therefore:

ψ+φ=atan2⁡(−R(3,2),−R(3,1))\psi+\varphi=\operatorname{atan2}\left(-R(3,2),-R(3,1)\right)ψ+φ=atan2(−R(3,2),−R(3,1))

05

MATLAB subprogram

A simple Matlab subprogram is given below.

R2TB
function [tb1 tb2] = R2TB(R)
%Convert rotation matrix R to Tait-Bryan angles tb1 and tb2
tol = 1e-12;
if abs(abs(R(1,3))-1) > tol
    theta1=asin(-R(1,3));
    ct=cos(theta1);
    psi1=atan2(R(1,2)/ct,R(1,1)/ct);
    phi1=atan2(R(2,3)/ct,R(3,3)/ct);
    tb1=[psi1 theta1 phi1];

    theta2=pi-theta1;
    ct=cos(theta2);
    psi2=atan2(R(1,2)/ct,R(1,1)/ct);
    phi2=atan2(R(2,3)/ct,R(3,3)/ct);
    tb2=[psi2 theta2 phi2];
elseif abs(R(1,3)+1) <= tol
    theta1=pi/2;
    phi1=0;
    psi1=atan2(R(3,2),R(3,1));
    tb1=[psi1 theta1 phi1];
    tb2=tb1;
elseif abs(R(1,3)-1) <= tol
    theta1=-pi/2;
    phi1=0;
    psi1=atan2(-R(3,2),-R(3,1));
    tb1=[psi1 theta1 phi1];
    tb2=tb1;
end

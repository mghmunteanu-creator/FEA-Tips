01

Motivation and hypotheses

StrainSmall
DisplacementLarge
MaterialLinear elastic
Stress statePlane stress

Sections 17.2 and 17.3 use the approximate additive strain update:

ei+1≈ei+Δe\boldsymbol{e}_{i+1}\approx\boldsymbol{e}_{i}+\Delta\boldsymbol{e}ei+1​≈ei​+Δe

Neither Hencky nor Biot strain is generally additive under successive finite deformations. Section 17.3 corrects the mismatch between the configurations associated with ei\boldsymbol{e}_{i}ei​ and Δe\Delta\boldsymbol{e}Δe, but the additive strain accumulation itself remains approximate.

The approach presented in this section benefited from an important contribution by Chiara: instead of accumulating the strain tensor approximately from one load step to the next, the total strain is evaluated directly from the current trial configuration.

Sections 17.4 and 17.5 originate from the incremental Updated Lagrangian developments of Sections 17.2 and 17.3, but all total kinematic quantities are evaluated with respect to the initial configuration. Therefore, they should not be regarded as strictly Updated Lagrangian formulations. The load steps are retained as a continuation strategy for the nonlinear solution rather than as successive kinematic reference configurations.

02

Total deformation gradient

Deformation gradients are naturally multiplicative. For two successive deformations:

Ftot=FincFprev\boldsymbol{F}_{\mathrm{tot}}=\boldsymbol{F}_{\mathrm{inc}}\boldsymbol{F}_{\mathrm{prev}}Ftot​=Finc​Fprev​

This multiplicative property is fundamentally different from an additive update of Hencky or Biot strain. In the present Q4 implementation, the total deformation gradient is evaluated directly from the initial and current trial configurations:

Ftot=(J0−1Jt)T\boxed{\boldsymbol{F}_{\mathrm{tot}}=\left(\boldsymbol{J}_{0}^{-1}\boldsymbol{J}_{t}\right)^{T}}Ftot​=(J0−1​Jt​)T​

Here, J0\boldsymbol{J}_{0}J0​ is the Jacobian of the initial configuration, while Jt\boldsymbol{J}_{t}Jt​ is the Jacobian of the current trial configuration. Consequently, no accumulated strain from previous load steps is needed.

03

Hencky and Biot strains in the current configuration

Using the definitions established in Section 17.1:

C=FtotTFtot\boldsymbol{C}=\boldsymbol{F}_{\mathrm{tot}}^{T}\boldsymbol{F}_{\mathrm{tot}}C=FtotT​Ftot​
U=C\boldsymbol{U}=\sqrt{\boldsymbol{C}}U=C​

The material Hencky strain tensor is:

EH=ln⁡U\boxed{\boldsymbol{E}_{H}=\ln\boldsymbol{U}}EH​=lnU​

The material Biot strain tensor is:

EB=U−I2\boxed{\boldsymbol{E}_{B}=\boldsymbol{U}-\boldsymbol{I}_{2}}EB​=U−I2​​

Uppercase E\boldsymbol{E}E denotes the strain tensor referred to the reference configuration. Lowercase e\boldsymbol{e}e denotes the same strain tensor expressed in the current configuration. From the polar decomposition:

Ftot=RU\boldsymbol{F}_{\mathrm{tot}}=\boldsymbol{R}\boldsymbol{U}Ftot​=RU

the rotation tensor is:

R=FtotU−1\boldsymbol{R}=\boldsymbol{F}_{\mathrm{tot}}\boldsymbol{U}^{-1}R=Ftot​U−1

Strain is a second-order tensor; therefore, its components are rotated according to:

e=RERT\boxed{\boldsymbol{e}=\boldsymbol{R}\boldsymbol{E}\boldsymbol{R}^{T}}e=RERT​

Thus, for Hencky and Biot strains:

eH=R(ln⁡U)RT\boldsymbol{e}_{H}=\boldsymbol{R}\left(\ln\boldsymbol{U}\right)\boldsymbol{R}^{T}eH​=R(lnU)RT
eB=R(U−I2)RT\boldsymbol{e}_{B}=\boldsymbol{R}\left(\boldsymbol{U}-\boldsymbol{I}_{2}\right)\boldsymbol{R}^{T}eB​=R(U−I2​)RT

This does not define a new strain measure. It is the same strain tensor expressed with the orientation of the current configuration, so that it can be used consistently with Cauchy stresses.

Accordingly, strnt stores strain components expressed in the current configuration, while sigmt stores Cauchy stress components expressed in the current configuration. The plot_strain and plot_stress routines display these stored current-configuration components without applying a further coordinate transformation.

04

Implemented strain–displacement approximation

For Hencky strain, determining the strain–displacement matrix B\boldsymbol{B}B is substantially more complicated than for the Euler–Almansi strain used in the previous UL formulations. The complete dependence is:

uel⟶Ftot⟶C⟶U⟶ln⁡U⟶R⟶e\boldsymbol{u}_{el}\longrightarrow\boldsymbol{F}_{\mathrm{tot}}\longrightarrow\boldsymbol{C}\longrightarrow\boldsymbol{U}\longrightarrow\ln\boldsymbol{U}\longrightarrow\boldsymbol{R}\longrightarrow\boldsymbol{e}uel​⟶Ftot​⟶C⟶U⟶lnU⟶R⟶e

Therefore, an exact analytical derivation of:

B=∂e∂uel\boldsymbol{B}=\frac{\partial\boldsymbol{e}}{\partial\boldsymbol{u}_{el}}B=∂uel​∂e​

and of all the corresponding geometric tangent terms becomes considerably more complicated than for Euler–Almansi strain.

The element internal force vector still uses the strain–displacement matrix B\boldsymbol{B}B derived in Sections 17.2 and 17.3. This matrix was obtained for the incremental formulation and is retained here as an approximation, although the total Hencky or Biot strain is now evaluated directly from the current trial configuration.

Consequently, the numerical differentiation introduced below produces a tangent stiffness matrix highly consistent with the implemented internal force vector, but it does not remove the approximation already contained in B\boldsymbol{B}B. Despite this approximation, the numerical tests presented below show excellent agreement with the reference solution for the present bending problem.

Direct evaluation of B\boldsymbol{B}B from the total strain tensor is another possible approach and may be investigated separately later.

05

Numerical tangent stiffness matrix

The numerical-differentiation strategy used in Section 16.3 can be reused almost unchanged. The element tangent stiffness matrix is:

kel=∂fel∂uel\boldsymbol{k}_{el}=\frac{\partial\boldsymbol{f}_{el}}{\partial\boldsymbol{u}_{el}}kel​=∂uel​∂fel​​

Using central differences, column iii is evaluated from:

kel(:,i)≈fel(uel+δci)−fel(uel−δci)2δ\boxed{\boldsymbol{k}_{el}(:,i)\approx\frac{\boldsymbol{f}_{el}\left(\boldsymbol{u}_{el}+\delta\boldsymbol{c}_{i}\right)-\boldsymbol{f}_{el}\left(\boldsymbol{u}_{el}-\delta\boldsymbol{c}_{i}\right)}{2\delta}}kel​(:,i)≈2δfel​(uel​+δci​)−fel​(uel​−δci​)​​

Here, ci\boldsymbol{c}_{i}ci​ is the eight-dimensional Cartesian basis vector associated with degree of freedom iii, and δ\deltaδ is the small displacement perturbation denoted by dd in MATLAB. It is distinct from the strain tensor ei\boldsymbol{e}_{i}ei​ at load step iii.

A Q4 element has eight nodal degrees of freedom. One unperturbed evaluation of fel\boldsymbol{f}_{el}fel​, eight evaluations at +δ+\delta+δ, and eight at −δ-\delta−δ are required. Therefore, one element tangent requires:

1+8+8=17\boxed{1+8+8=17}1+8+8=17​

The central-difference part of stiff.m is:

MATLAB — central numerical tangent
fel_el
fel0=fel;

for ipert=1:8
    sel(ipert)=sel(ipert)+dd;
    fel_el
    felp=fel;

    sel(ipert)=sel(ipert)-2*dd;
    fel_el
    felm=fel;

    sel(ipert)=sel(ipert)+dd;

    kel(:,ipert)=(felp-felm)/(2*dd);
end

The formulation implemented in the fel_el subroutine is quite different from that used in Section 16.3, but the numerical procedure used to obtain the tangent stiffness matrix is practically unchanged. Only the expression of the element internal force vector changes.

06

Computational cost

The price of this general procedure is computational time. Each Newton iteration is substantially more expensive because the element internal force vector must be evaluated 17 times for each element, with all Gauss-point calculations repeated at every evaluation.

An attempted precomputation of the Gauss-point geometry produced negligible speed improvement. It was therefore abandoned in favor of the simpler and more transparent program.

Two possible acceleration strategies are worth mentioning. The sixteen perturbed evaluations of the element internal force vector are mutually independent and could therefore be evaluated in parallel. Also, the general sqrtm and logm operations for the 2×22\times22×2 matrices could be replaced by specialised spectral expressions based on eigenvalues and eigenvectors. These optimisations do not change the formulation, but they make the program less transparent and are therefore not used here.

Section 17.4 requires far fewer Newton iterations than Section 17.3 and becomes much less sensitive to the number of load steps. Its displacement results show excellent agreement with the ANSYS reference. The price is a much larger computational cost per iteration because every tangent column requires two additional internal-force evaluations.

The comparison with ANSYS is intentionally based on displacements. Stress recovery and nodal averaging procedures differ between ANSYS and the MATLAB programs, so a direct comparison of nodal stress extrema would mix formulation effects with post-processing differences.

08

Additional strain-map test

For an additional bending test at load step 5, the computed Hencky axial strain extrema are:

εxmin⁡=−0.062736εxmax⁡=0.062338\varepsilon_x^{\min}=-0.062736\qquad\varepsilon_x^{\max}=0.062338εxmin​=−0.062736εxmax​=0.062338

The agreement with the reference solution remains excellent even when the local strains are appreciable. For this bending benchmark, the deformation remains relatively close to a locally coaxial state. This observation helps explain the remarkable accuracy of the retained B\boldsymbol{B}B approximation here, but it should not be interpreted as a universal result for arbitrary finite deformations.

10

Conclusions

  1. The total deformation gradient is evaluated directly from the initial and current trial configurations. No strain tensor accumulated from previous load steps is required.
  2. Hencky and Biot strains are obtained directly from the right stretch tensor U\boldsymbol{U}U, avoiding the approximate additive accumulation used in Sections 17.2 and 17.3.
  3. Hencky and Biot strains are first evaluated from the right stretch tensor U\boldsymbol{U}U and are therefore referred to the orientation of the reference configuration. Since Cauchy stresses are expressed in the current configuration, the strain tensor is subsequently rotated by R\boldsymbol{R}R so that strains and stresses are expressed with respect to the same orientation.
  4. Because of this direct total-strain evaluation and rotation, an analytical derivation of the associated strain–displacement matrix and tangent stiffness becomes considerably more involved than in the Euler–Almansi formulation.
  5. Numerical differentiation of fel\boldsymbol{f}_{el}fel​ avoids the analytical tangent derivation and demonstrates the generality of the method already used in Section 16.3: the numerical algorithm is retained while the internal-force formulation changes.
  6. The procedure substantially improves convergence and reduces sensitivity to load-step size, but computational time per iteration increases strongly because 17 internal-force evaluations are required for every element tangent.
